<a id="section-02"></a>

## 02. Supporting detail

<a id="section-03"></a>

## 03. Scope and interpretation

These minutes explain the transition and its rationale. They do not act as a perpetual roster of current personnel or establish who is on call at a later time.

<a id="section-04"></a>

## 04. Business and service context

Design authorship, service accountability, and shift coverage are related but distinct responsibilities. A dated design can correctly name a team that is no longer responsible for day-to-day operation. A transfer record explains a change at its effective date, while a current directory resolves people and contact details. Retaining the original dates and attribution allows investigations to reconstruct responsibility without retroactively changing what an earlier reader would have known.

<a id="section-05"></a>

## 05. Record and follow-up

These minutes preserve the distinction between discussion, accepted decisions, and work assigned for later completion. An action item remains open until the responsible record provides evidence of delivery or an authorized decision changes its disposition. Readers should use the subsequent policy, architecture decision, release, or verification record for the authority specific to that stage. The meeting record supplies the rationale and unresolved questions known at the time.

<a id="section-06"></a>

## 06. Application example

A handover discussion can assign responsibility for maintaining a runbook while leaving authorship of the original architecture with its historical team. Those statements are compatible. The receiving operating model needs enough context to continue unresolved actions, but the record should not imply that the receiving team designed every inherited component. Later questions about a specific responder must use the applicable roster rather than infer personal responsibility from attendance at this meeting.
